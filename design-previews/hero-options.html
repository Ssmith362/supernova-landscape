Option B

Split editorial

Copy on the night field, photo bleeding off the right edge at full height — no gutter, no margin, so the two panels read as one composition. Type contrast is perfect because the words never sit on the photo. The proof chip straddles the seam, which is what stitches the halves together.

Option C

Overlapping card

Keeps your existing panorama crop exactly as shot, then fixes the seam by making the copy card overlap the photo's lower edge. Two layers that interlock read as one object; two layers that merely touch read as a mistake. Gold hairline along the card's top edge marks the join deliberately.

Option D

Framed panorama — type crosses the frame

The photograph sits in an inset panel pushed to the right, and the headline block overlaps its lower-left corner. Type crossing a picture boundary is the oldest trick in editorial design and the single most reliable way to make copy and image feel like one artefact. The panorama stays a panorama, which is what it wants to be.

Respects the 2.4:1 cropMost distinctive / least generic Reads as a brand, not a templatePhoto is framed, not immersive

Option A2 · recommended build

Cinematic + service rail — the rotating hero, done without the damage

Your live Webflow site rotates four different headlines through the hero. That is the part that hurts. This keeps the movement you liked and drops the harm: the H1 and the CTAs never move, only the photograph behind them does, and the four service names sit in a visible, clickable rail with a progress bar. Auto-advances every 7 seconds, pauses the moment you hover or tab into it, and holds still entirely under prefers-reduced-motion. Every label is a real link to that service page — so it is navigation with pictures, not a carousel.
